Information Theory: What Does a Coin Flip Tell You?

Start from one covered coin and rebuild Shannon's whole theory of information, one why-does-this-have-to-be-true at a time.

Start with something you can hold in your hand.

You flip a fair coin and slap your palm over it. You don’t know how it landed. Then you lift your hand and look. Nothing about the coin changed, but something about you did. That change has a size, and measuring it is the whole subject of this post.

The size is one bit. Both faces have probability 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, and the information you gained is −log⁡212=1-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1−log2​21​=1.

Hold on to that number. By the end, the same idea will explain zip files, phone calls, spy-proof ciphers and probes leaving the solar system.

Step 1: a coin, a word, a number#

Information theory is the mathematical study of how to quantify, store and communicate a specific, mathematically defined kind of information. Claude Shannon founded and formalized it in the 1940s. Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley had made early contributions in the 1920s. Today it sits where mathematics, statistics and computer science overlap.

Let’s build it from the coin.

  1. Before you look, you lack a certain amount of information about the outcome.
  2. Looking removes that lack. You have gained information.
  3. Shannon’s word for the lack is entropy. Before you look, the entropy is 1 bit.
  4. After you look, the entropy is zero, because you gained exactly the one bit you were missing.

Now scale up. Suppose someone sends you 1000 bits, each a 0 or a 1. If you already know every value before it arrives, the transmission carries no information at all. If instead each bit is independent and equally likely to be 0 or 1, the transmission carries 1000 bits of information. The unit is also called the shannon.

Information, in this picture, is the resolution of uncertainty. A message you could already predict resolves nothing.

Why does a rarer event have to carry more information?#

Suppose the coin is a bad one, and heads comes up almost every time. Someone tells you it landed heads. Are you surprised?

No. And if you’re not surprised, you didn’t learn much.

So the information in a single outcome should depend on how improbable it was. The rarer the event, the bigger the surprise. This quantity is called self-information, or surprisal:

I(x)=−log⁡2p(x) I(x) = -\log_2 p(x) I(x)=−log2​p(x)

Here is what that looks like for a few probabilities.

Probability p(x)p(x)p(x) Surprisal in bits
12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ 111
14\tfrac{1}{4}41​ 222
18\tfrac{1}{8}81​ 333

Each time the probability halves, the surprise goes up by one bit. Your coin flip is the first row.

Why does there have to be a logarithm?#

You might wonder why the formula uses a logarithm at all. The people who came before Shannon can tell us.

Ralph Hartley, writing in 1928, treated information as something measurable: the receiver’s ability to tell one sequence of symbols from every other. Suppose there are SSS possible symbols and a transmission contains nnn of them. Then there are SnS^nSn possible sequences, and Hartley defined the information as:

H=log⁡Sn=nlog⁡S H = \log S^n = n \log S H=logSn=nlogS

Ask yourself what we want from a measure of information. A message twice as long should carry twice as much. But the number of possible sequences multiplies as messages get longer. A logarithm turns that multiplication into addition. That is the reason it’s there.

Hartley’s natural unit was the decimal digit, which is sometimes called the hartley in his honor.

Four years earlier, in 1924, Nyquist’s paper on telegraph speed had already put a number on how fast “intelligence” can be sent down a line. His relation was W=Klog⁡mW = K \log mW=Klogm. Here WWW is the speed of transmission, mmm is the number of distinct voltage levels available at each time step, and KKK is a constant.

Look closely at both formulas. Every symbol, every voltage level, is treated as equally likely. That’s the gap Shannon filled.

Average the surprise: entropy#

Real sources aren’t uniform. Some symbols are common and some are rare. So how surprised should you expect to be, on average?

Take each symbol’s surprisal, weight it by how often the symbol occurs, and add everything up. The result is the Shannon entropy, the expected value of the information content of the source’s symbols:

H(X)=−∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x) H(X) = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log_2 p(x) H(X)=−x∑​p(x)log2​p(x)

Entropy is the average surprisal of a source.

By convention, a term of the form plog⁡pp \log pplogp counts as zero when p=0p = 0p=0. The article behind this post justifies that convention for any logarithm base.

Intuitively, entropy measures the uncertainty about a random variable when all you know is its distribution. High entropy means the outcomes are spread out evenly, so the result is hard to predict.

Why is the uniform case the maximum?#

Try the extreme cases.

  • If one outcome is certain, there is nothing to learn, and the entropy is zero.
  • If all nnn outcomes are equally likely, you can’t lean on any of them, and the entropy is at its largest.

That second case is a theorem. For a source with nnn possible symbols:

H(X)≤log⁡n H(X) \le \log n H(X)≤logn

Equality holds exactly when every symbol has the same probability. The maximum marks the most unpredictable state a source with nnn symbols can be in.

There’s a related fact about whole messages. If the symbols are independent and identically distributed, a message of length nnn carries nHnHnH bits in total. If the symbols share a distribution but aren’t independent, the entropy of the message is less than nHnHnH.

The two-outcome case#

Now go back to the coin, but let it be biased. One outcome has probability ppp, so the other has 1−p1-p1−p. This is the binary entropy function:

Hb(p)=−plog⁡2p−(1−p)log⁡2(1−p) H_b(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2 (1-p) Hb​(p)=−plog2​p−(1−p)log2​(1−p)

It peaks at exactly 1 bit when p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5, which is the fair coin. That’s the point of maximum uncertainty. Push ppp toward 0 or 1 and the entropy drops toward zero.

Two variables at once#

So far we have measured one random variable. Communication involves at least two: what was sent and what arrived. Here are the tools for that.

Joint entropy is the entropy of the pair (X,Y)(X, Y)(X,Y) treated as a single variable. If XXX and YYY are independent, their joint entropy is just the sum of their individual entropies. Take a chess piece as an example. Let XXX be its row and YYY its column. The joint entropy of row and column is then the entropy of the piece’s position. Don’t confuse this with cross-entropy, despite the similar notation.

Conditional entropy is the average uncertainty left in XXX once you know YYY. It is also called the equivocation of XXX about YYY. There are two definitions in circulation, one conditioned on YYY as a whole variable and one conditioned on a single value of it. The first is more common, so watch which one a text means. A basic property ties it back to joint entropy:

H(X∣Y)=H(X,Y)−H(Y) H(X \mid Y) = H(X, Y) - H(Y) H(X∣Y)=H(X,Y)−H(Y)

Mutual information measures how much you can learn about one variable by observing another. In communication, it is what you’d maximize between the signal sent and the signal received. Its most useful reading is a saving:

I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y) I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y)

That is, knowing YYY lets you save an average of I(X;Y)I(X;Y)I(X;Y) bits when encoding XXX, compared with not knowing YYY. It’s symmetric, so I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)I(X;Y)=I(Y;X).

There’s a second way to read it, using the divergence we’re about to meet. Mutual information is the average shift from the prior distribution on XXX to its posterior once you’re told YYY. Equivalently, it is the divergence from the product of the two marginal distributions to the actual joint distribution. It is also closely related to the log-likelihood ratio test and Pearson’s χ2\chi^2χ2 test, so it can serve as a statistic for whether two variables are independent. Its pointwise cousin is called pointwise mutual information.

Being wrong costs bits: Kullback–Leibler divergence#

Here is a question. What if you compress data assuming one distribution, qqq, but the true distribution is ppp?

The answer is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, also called relative entropy or information gain:

DKL(p∥q)=∑xp(x)log⁡p(x)q(x) D_{\text{KL}}(p \parallel q) = \sum_{x} p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} DKL​(p∥q)=x∑​p(x)logq(x)p(x)​

It counts the average number of extra bits per datum that your wrong assumption costs you.

Picture two people watching a number drawn at random. Alice knows the true distribution ppp. Bob believes it is qqq. On average, Bob will be more surprised than Alice. The divergence is the expected value of Bob’s surprisal minus Alice’s, in bits if the logarithm is base 2. It measures how unnecessarily surprised a wrong prior makes you.

People sometimes call it a distance, but it isn’t a true metric. It is not symmetric, and it doesn’t satisfy the triangle inequality, which makes it a semi-quasimetric. It also links information theory to hypothesis testing: it is the optimal error exponent in asymmetric hypothesis testing.

One-way information#

Mutual information is symmetric. But causes come before effects, and some questions care about direction. Directed information, a term coined by James Massey, measures how many bits flow causally from one process XnX^nXn to another process YnY^nYn:

I(Xn→Yn)=∑i=1nI(Xi;Yi∣Yi−1) I(X^n \to Y^n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} I\left(X^i ; Y_i \mid Y^{i-1}\right) I(Xn→Yn)=i=1∑n​I(Xi;Yi​∣Yi−1)

Unlike mutual information, it is not symmetric. It matters wherever causality does: channels with feedback, discrete memoryless networks with feedback, gambling and compression with causal side information, real-time control settings and statistical physics.

The family of measures keeps growing. Rényi entropy and Tsallis entropy generalize entropy. Differential entropy extends it to continuous distributions. Pragmatic information has been proposed as a measure of how much information was actually used in making a decision.

Sources: where the symbols come from#

Any process that produces a stream of messages counts as a source. A memoryless source emits messages that are independent and identically distributed. Ergodicity and stationarity are weaker conditions. All of these sources are stochastic, and each idea is well studied on its own outside information theory.

The information rate is the average entropy per symbol. For a memoryless source, that is simply the entropy of each symbol. For a stationary process, it is the conditional entropy of a symbol given everything before it:

r=lim⁡n→∞H(Xn∣Xn−1,Xn−2,…,X1) r = \lim_{n \to \infty} H(X_n \mid X_{n-1}, X_{n-2}, \ldots, X_1) r=n→∞lim​H(Xn​∣Xn−1​,Xn−2​,…,X1​)

For a process that isn’t necessarily stationary, the rate is the limit of the joint entropy per symbol:

r=lim⁡n→∞1nH(X1,X2,…,Xn) r = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n) r=n→∞lim​n1​H(X1​,X2​,…,Xn​)

For stationary sources, the two definitions agree.

This lets us talk about the “rate” or “entropy” of a language, such as English prose. That rate is tied to the language’s redundancy and to how well it can be compressed.

Two jobs for codes: squeeze, then protect#

Coding theory looks for explicit methods, called codes, that make communication more efficient and less error-prone, ideally close to the theoretical limit. Using a statistical description of the data, information theory tells you how many bits it takes to describe the data. That number is the entropy of the source.

Codes split into two jobs.

  1. Source coding (data compression). There are two versions of the problem. In lossless compression, the data must be reconstructed exactly. In lossy compression, you spend only as many bits as needed to reconstruct the data within a chosen level of fidelity, measured by a distortion function. That second branch is called rate–distortion theory.
  2. Channel coding (error correction). Compression squeezes out as much redundancy as it can. An error-correcting code then puts back just the right kind of redundancy, so data crosses a noisy channel efficiently and faithfully.

The split into compression and transmission is justified by the source–channel separation theorems, which make bits a universal currency for information in many settings.

There is a catch. Those theorems hold only for one sender talking to one receiver. Add more transmitters (a multiple-access channel), more receivers (a broadcast channel), intermediate helpers (a relay channel) or a general network, and compressing first and transmitting second may no longer be optimal.

For sources and channels that aren’t stationary or ergodic, information-spectrum methods describe the coding limits using the asymptotic distributions of information density. A related question, channel resolvability, asks what rate the channel inputs need in order to approximate a target output distribution. Han and Sergio Verdú connected that problem to coding theorems for general channels. Hayashi later derived general formulas linking resolvability and identification capacity, and applied them to secrecy analysis for the wiretap channel.

Why does noise not make communication impossible?#

Now the central puzzle. A channel often fails to reproduce a signal exactly. Noise, silences and other corruption degrade it. You might expect that the only way to be more reliable is to send more slowly, forever.

Shannon proved otherwise in 1948.

Set up a discrete channel. The messages sent come from a space XXX, and the messages received come from a space YYY. The conditional distribution p(y∣x)p(y \mid x)p(y∣x) is a fixed property of the channel, and it encodes the nature of the noise. You don’t control it. You do control f(x)f(x)f(x), the distribution of messages you choose to send. Together these fix the joint distribution of XXX and YYY.

You want to send as much signal as possible, and the right measure for that is mutual information. Its maximum over your choices is the channel capacity:

C=max⁡fI(X;Y) C = \max_{f} I(X;Y) C=fmax​I(X;Y)

Below capacity, reliable communication is possible. Above it, it is impossible.

Here is the exact statement, the noisy-channel coding theorem. Take any information rate R<CR < CR<C (usually measured in bits per symbol) and any error tolerance ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0. For large enough block length NNN, there exists a code of length NNN and rate at least RRR, together with a decoding algorithm, so that the maximal probability of block error is at most ε\varepsilonε. You can always transmit with arbitrarily small block error. But for any rate R>CR > CR>C, that is impossible.

In the limit of many uses of the channel, the achievable rate equals the capacity. And capacity depends only on the statistics of the channel.

Finding practical codes that reach that promise was a different story. It took many years to discover the methods Shannon’s work had proven were possible.

Three channels to try by hand#

  • A binary symmetric channel flips each input bit with probability ppp. Its capacity is 1−Hb(p)1 - H_b(p)1−Hb​(p) bits per channel use.
  • A binary erasure channel never flips a bit. It sometimes replaces it with a third symbol, eee, meaning that bit is completely lost. With erasure probability ppp, its capacity is 1−p1 - p1−p bits per channel use.
  • A continuous-time analog channel with Gaussian noise is governed by the Shannon–Hartley theorem.

Plug in a few values for the first two:

Binary symmetric p=0p = 0p=0 111
Binary symmetric p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5 000

The middle row is worth pausing on. A channel that flips every bit half the time carries nothing, because Hb(0.5)=1H_b(0.5) = 1Hb​(0.5)=1 uses up the whole bit.

Real channels often have memory, meaning the output at time iii depends on the past. Then the capacity is given by the mutual information rate when no feedback is available. With feedback, it is given by the directed information rate. Without feedback, the two are equal.

How Shannon put it all together#

Now the history, in order. It reads differently once you know where it lands.

When Who What
1924 Harry Nyquist Paper on telegraph speed quantifying “intelligence” and “line speed”: W=Klog⁡mW = K \log mW=Klogm
1928 Ralph Hartley Paper treating information as a measurable quantity: H=nlog⁡SH = n \log SH=nlogS
1939 Claude Shannon Outlines some early ideas in a letter to Vannevar Bush
1940 Alan Turing Uses similar ideas in the statistical analysis of the German Enigma ciphers
End of 1944 Claude Shannon Substantially completes the work at Bell Labs
July and October 1948 Claude Shannon Publishes “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” in the Bell System Technical Journal
1960s Rolf Landauer Key contributions on the link between information-theoretic and thermodynamic entropy

Before 1948, the information-theoretic ideas developed at Bell Labs were limited, and they all assumed events of equal probability. Much of the mathematics for events of different probabilities had been developed for thermodynamics by Ludwig Boltzmann and J. Willard Gibbs. Landauer’s later contributions explore how information-theoretic entropy and thermodynamic entropy connect.

What Shannon’s paper did was present communication as a statistical process. It brought a whole set of ideas at once:

  • The entropy and redundancy of a source, and the source coding theorem.
  • Mutual information and the capacity of a noisy channel, including the noisy-channel coding theorem’s promise of perfect, loss-free communication.
  • The Shannon–Hartley law for the capacity of a Gaussian channel.
  • The bit, a new way of seeing the most fundamental unit of information.

The paper brought the field instant worldwide attention. The historian James Gleick rated it the most important development of 1948, judging it even more profound and fundamental than the transistor. Shannon came to be known as the father of information theory.

The one place bits and secrets meet#

The same mathematics governs codes and ciphers. Cryptography and cryptanalysis lean heavily on it.

Turing’s unit of information, the ban, was used in the Ultra project. That project broke the German Enigma machine code and hastened the end of World War II in Europe. Shannon himself defined the unicity distance. Based on the redundancy of the plaintext, it gives the minimum amount of ciphertext needed to guarantee that a message can be deciphered in only one way.

Information theory suggests that keeping a secret is harder than it looks. Brute-force attacks can break systems built on asymmetric-key algorithms, and most commonly used symmetric-key methods such as block ciphers. Their security rests on an assumption: no known attack can break them in a practical amount of time.

Information-theoretic security is different. Methods like the one-time pad are immune to brute force, and here is why. The mutual information between plaintext and ciphertext, given the key, is positive, so the message gets through. The unconditional mutual information between plaintext and ciphertext stays at zero. An eavesdropper who has the ciphertext but not the key can’t improve a guess about the plaintext at all.

Even a perfect method can be misused. The Venona project cracked Soviet one-time pads because key material had been reused.

Randomness is a related trap. The pseudorandom number generators found in programming libraries are almost universally unsuited to cryptography, because they can’t escape the deterministic nature of computers. Cryptographically secure generators improve on them, but they still need random seeds that come from outside the software. Those seeds can be distilled using extractors, if that is done carefully. The measure of sufficient randomness for an extractor is min-entropy, which is related to Shannon entropy through Rényi entropy. The important warning is that a variable with high Shannon entropy is not automatically good enough for an extractor, and so not automatically good enough for cryptography.

Everywhere the idea landed#

Information theory began in telecommunication and spread. Source coding gave us data compression, such as ZIP files. Channel coding gave us error detection and correction, such as DSL. Its impact has been crucial to the Voyager missions to deep space, the invention of the compact disc, the feasibility of mobile phones, and the development of the Internet and artificial intelligence.

The list of other homes is long. It includes statistical inference, neurobiology, perception, signal processing, linguistics, and the evolution and function of molecular codes in bioinformatics. It also reaches thermal physics, molecular dynamics, black holes and quantum computing. It shows up in information retrieval, intelligence gathering, plagiarism detection, pattern recognition and anomaly detection. And it has been applied to the analysis of music, art creation, imaging system design, the study of outer space, the dimensionality of space, and epistemology.

A few of these are worth a closer look.

  • Seismic oil exploration was one of the earliest commercial applications. The theory made it possible to strip unwanted noise off the desired seismic signal. Together with digital signal processing, it greatly improved resolution and image clarity compared with the earlier analog methods.
  • Semiotics. The semioticians Doede Nauta and Winfried Nöth both considered Charles Sanders Peirce to have created a theory of information in his work on signs. Umberto Eco and Ferruccio Rossi-Landi used redundancy and code control to explain ideology as message transmission. A dominant social class sends its message in signs so redundant that, out of the competing messages, only one gets decoded.
  • Cognitive science. Quantitative information-theoretic methods have been applied to the binding problem in cognitive neuroscience. Examples include Gerald Edelman and Giulio Tononi’s functional clustering model and dynamic core hypothesis, Tononi’s integrated information theory of consciousness (which uses effective information), and Karl Friston’s free energy principle, an information-theoretical measure. The Bayesian brain hypothesis belongs to the same family.
  • Network physiology uses mutual information, transfer entropy and co-information to study how physiological systems exchange and integrate information as a network. It detects coupling strength, direction, synergy and redundancy. Applications span brain–body information transfer, cardio-respiratory, cardio-muscular and cortico-muscular interactions, brain-wave interactions and functional brain networks, and network physiology in extreme environments.
  • Elsewhere: the search for extraterrestrial intelligence, the black-hole information paradox, bioinformatics and gambling.

One more distinction keeps the picture honest. Fungible information is information for which the means of encoding doesn’t matter. Classical information theorists and computer scientists mostly care about this kind, which is sometimes called speakable information.

Where the tidy picture stops#

Three caveats are worth carrying away.

  1. The bit is only a universal currency for one sender and one receiver. Once networks enter, compress-then-transmit may no longer be optimal.
  2. Capacity is a promise, not a recipe. The theorem says good codes exist. Finding them took many years.
  3. High entropy isn’t the same as usable randomness. Shannon entropy alone can’t tell you whether a source is good enough for cryptography.

Now return to the covered coin. One fair flip is one bit. Weight each outcome by its surprise, and you get entropy. Ask how much of that survives a noisy wire, and you get capacity. And ask how to squeeze and protect a message on the way, and you get all of coding theory.
