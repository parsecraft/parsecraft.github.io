The Jargon File: How a Lab's Private Slang Became a Style Guide

Trace a shared text file from a Stanford directory to the Chicago Manual of Style and see what survives when the machines that hosted a culture are switched off.

Here is a problem that sounds trivial until you try to solve it. A community of programmers has spent years coining words for things that have no other name. Those words live in people’s heads and on a handful of specific machines. Then the machines get switched off. How do you keep the vocabulary alive?

Let’s just trace through what actually happened, one copy of one file at a time.

Step 0: the vocabulary before the file#

The story starts before any file exists. In the 1950s, the Tech Model Railroad Club (TMRC) at MIT produced early hackers on the TX-0 and PDP-1 computers. That is where the word hacker emerged, along with much of the ethic, the philosophy and the nomenclature.

Some of that nomenclature was already old when the file began. The words frob, foo and mung are believed to date back to the early 1950s at the club. Peter Samson wrote them down in the 1959 Dictionary of the TMRC Language. So the slang had a written ancestor twenty-odd years before anyone typed the first line of the file we’re about to follow.

Step 1: one file, one machine#

The file proper begins in 1975, when Raphael Finkel made it at Stanford. Retroactively it’s called “Jargon-1”, or “the File”. The name it lived under until the Stanford AI Lab (SAIL) computer was finally shut down in 1991 looks like this:

AIWORD.RF[UP,DOC]
   |         |
   |         +-- a system directory on the WAITS operating system:
   |             "User Program DOCumentation"
   +-- "AI words", plus Finkel's initials

Notice what the name assumes. It’s a file of AI words, on one lab’s machine. Its scope is built into its filename.

One small piece of trivia belongs here. Nobody at the time called it “the File”. Its users said “AIWORD” or “the Jargon file”. The capitalized “the File” was coined later by Eric Raymond.

Step 2: the copy that changed the file’s scope#

In 1976, Mark Crispin saw an announcement about the file on the SAIL computer and FTPed a copy to the MIT AI Lab. He noticed that it was hardly limited to “AI words” and filed it under a name that says so:

SAIL side:   AIWORD.RF[UP,DOC]
MIT side:    AI:MRC;SAIL JARGON
             |  |   |
             |  |   +-- the file: "SAIL JARGON"
             |  +------ directory "MRC" (Crispin's)
             +--------- the AI Lab computer

This is a fork, and the trace ends up with two live copies. Finkel dropped out of active participation shortly afterwards, and Don Woods became the SAIL contact. From then on the file was kept in duplicate at SAIL and MIT, with periodic resynchronizations. It grew by fits and starts, and Richard Stallman was a prominent contributor. He added many MIT and ITS coinages.

A side detail from those additions is that the Incompatible Timesharing System got its name to distinguish it from another early MIT operating system, the Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS).

Step 3: from shared file to paper#

In 1981, a hacker named Charles Spurgeon got a large chunk of the file published in Stewart Brand’s CoEvolution Quarterly (issue 29, pages 26–35). It was illustrated by Phil Wadler and Guy Steele, including a couple of Steele’s Crunchly cartoons, and it seems to have been the file’s first appearance on paper.

The bigger step came in 1983, when Guy Steele edited a late version of Jargon-1 into a mass-market book, The Hacker’s Dictionary (Harper & Row). It carried commentary added for general readers, and it included all of Steele’s Crunchly cartoons. The other Jargon-1 editors (Finkel, Woods and Crispin) contributed, as did Stallman and Geoff Goodfellow. The file’s history calls this book “Steele-1983” and its six contributors “the Steele-1983 coauthors”.

Step 4: why the file froze#

Right after the book appeared, the file effectively stopped changing. The original plan was to freeze it temporarily so the book could be produced. External conditions turned that freeze permanent. Here is the dependency chain that did it:

late 1970s   funding cuts at the AI Lab
             -> administration prefers vendor-supported hardware and
                proprietary software over homebrew
             -> MIT AI work moves to dedicated Lisp machines
             -> best people lured to startups (Route 128, Silicon Valley)
             -> the startups build Lisp machines FOR MIT
             -> central MIT-AI computer becomes a TWENEX system,
                no longer an ITS host for the AI hackers

by 1980      Stanford AI Lab has effectively ceased to exist
             (the SAIL computer lives on as a CS department resource until 1991)
             Stanford becomes a major TWENEX site: more than a dozen TOPS-20 systems

mid-1980s    most of the interesting software work is on BSD Unix

April 1983   DEC cancels the Jupiter project
             -> PDP-10 culture, which nourished the file, takes a death-blow

Every arrow in that chain removes a machine or a community the file depended on. Steele-1983 was partly a monument to what its authors believed was a dying tradition, and none of them realized how far its influence would reach.

That influence came even though the content had dated by the mid-1980s. Softcopies from the ARPANET and the book itself kept circulating in cultures far from MIT. The file, together with related material such as the AI Koans in Appendix A, came to be seen as a sort of sacred epic, a hacker-culture Matter of Britain about the Knights of the Lab. It stopped being a living document and became an icon, and it stayed essentially untouched for seven years.

Step 5: rewriting the file (1990)#

A new revision began in 1990. Its design is easy to state as a merge:

new_file  = late_jargon_1
            - obsolete_pdp10_entries         # dropped after consulting Steele-1983's editors
new_file += ~80% of steele_1983              # minus some framing material and a very few
                                             # entries now of historical interest only
new_file += entries from wider cultures:
            Usenet (C and Unix communities)  # more than half of all entries
            IBM PC programmers, Amiga fans, Mac enthusiasts, IBM mainframe world

The goal changed along with the inputs. The new version aimed to cover all the technical computing cultures where “the true hacker-nature” shows up, not only AI or PDP-10 hackers. Eric Raymond maintained it with help from Guy Steele. He is the credited editor of the print version, The New Hacker’s Dictionary (MIT Press, 1991).

The archived versions give a compact timeline:

Version Year What it is
1.0.3 1981 Oldest recovered version
1.5.0 1983 The Hacker’s Dictionary, Steele’s last
2.1.1 1990 Raymond’s first
2.9.6 1991 First edition of The New Hacker’s Dictionary
3.0.0 1993 Second edition
4.0.0 1996 Third edition
4.4.7 2003 Raymond’s last
5.0.1 2012 Volunteer update after Raymond, the last major revision

The trade-off Raymond took on#

Some of the changes were controversial. Early critics accused Raymond of shifting the file’s focus to Unix hacker culture, away from the older cultures it came from. His answer was that the nature of hacking had changed, and that the file should report on hacker culture instead of trying to enshrine it.

After the second edition, he was accused of adding terms that reflected his own politics and vocabulary. His reply was that candidate entries are checked for live use, so they are not “just the private coinage of one or two people”.

The third edition (MIT Press, 1996) was revised again to include terminology from the nascent public Internet and the World Wide Web.

A worked example: the -P convention#

To see what an entry looks like, take one of the file’s best-known conventions. In Lisp, a function that returns a yes-or-no answer is called a predicate, and by convention its name gets a p appended. Hackers carried this into speech. You turn a statement into a question by adding “P”, which is pronounced out loud as a separate syllable, “Pee”. Here is the standard exchange, traced:

Q: "Foodp?"     ; do you want food?
A: "T!"         ; Lisp's true: yes
A: "Nil."       ; also null: no, I don't want food

The habit is most common at MIT and Berkeley and among computer scientists working in artificial intelligence, which leans heavily on Lisp. After the file immortalized it, the usage spread to some younger users who wanted to belong to the classical Internet community. It is a small example of how the file works as a mechanism. It records a usage, and by recording it, it exports it.

Why write a dictionary of hackers at all?#

There was also a public-relations problem, and it sits in the file’s history from the start. In the early 1990s especially, many news stories portrayed hackers as lawbreakers with no respect for other people’s privacy or property. The print editions were partly a response. They set out to celebrate hacker culture, to give younger and future hackers a repository of their history, and above all to show the general public a positive picture of it.

The book is particularly noted for trying to preserve a distinction: a hacker is a consummate programmer, and a cracker is a computer criminal. Both the London Review of Books and MIT Technology Review remarked on this, even without reviewing the book in detail. The Encyclopedia of New Media (Steve Jones, 2002) went further and named the defense of the word “hacker” as a motivating factor for both Steele’s and Raymond’s editions. Raymond wanted to show some of the positive values of hacker culture, its sense of humor in particular. Love of humorous wordplay runs through the culture, so a slang dictionary is a natural vehicle for that goal.

What the outside world did with it#

Despite its tongue-in-cheek approach, the dictionary got cited as a serious reference.

  • Dictionaries and style guides. The Oxford English Dictionary has used The New Hacker’s Dictionary as a source for computer-related neologisms. The Chicago Manual of Style, beginning with its 15th edition (2003), defers for “computer writing” to the logical quotation style recommended in the dictionary’s essay “Hacker Writing Style”. It cites the dictionary for nothing else, and the 16th edition (2010) does the same. The National Geographic Style Manual, the house style of the society’s publications, lists the dictionary among only 8 specialized dictionaries out of 22 total sources on which it is based. It does not say what it drew from it.
  • Books. The file is cited for its definitions and essays in works on hacker history, cyberpunk subculture, computer jargon and online style. They include Wired Style (1999), Mark Dery’s Flame Wars (1994) and Escape Velocity (2007), and Beyond Cyberpunk! (1991). A more recent example is David Livingstone’s Transhumanism: The History of a Dangerous Idea (2015).
  • Media. Time used the second edition as the basis for an article on online culture in the November 1995 inaugural “Time Digital” department. Newsweek used it as a primary source for a major article on the Internet and its history, and quoted entries in a sidebar. The dictionary was cited by name on the front page of The Wall Street Journal. MTV’s This Week in Rock used excerpts in its “CyberStuff” segments, and Computing Reviews put one of its definitions on the cover of its December 1991 issue.

One entry goes to court#

On October 23, 2003, the dictionary turned up in a legal case. SCO Group cited the 1996 edition’s definition of FUD (fear, uncertainty and doubt) in a filing in SCO Group, Inc. v. International Business Machines Corp. That definition dwells on questionable IBM business practices. In response, Raymond added SCO to the entry in a revised copy of the file, because he felt SCO’s own practices deserved similar criticism.

Reception#

Reviewers tended to say the same thing in different ways, that the book was fun and authentic.

  • PC Magazine (1984) judged The Hacker’s Dictionary better than most computer-humor books and praised its authenticity to “hard-core programmers’ conversations”, especially the MIT and Stanford slang.
  • William Safire of The New York Times called Raymond’s 1991 edition a “sprightly lexicon” and recommended it as a nerdy gift that holiday season. The recommendation reappeared in his “On Language” column in mid-October 1992.
  • Hugh Kenner, in Byte, suggested your reading of it should be “severely timed” if you hope to get any work done.
  • Mondo 2000 praised it as “slippery, elastic fun with language” and called it a de facto ethnography of the early years of hacker culture.
  • Positive reviews also appeared in IEEE Spectrum, New Scientist, PC World, Science and, repeatedly, Wired. The third edition drew more coverage, including Wired (August 1996) and People (October 21, 1996).

The game designer Steve Jackson, writing for Boing Boing, had mixed feelings. He called the essay “A Portrait of J. Random Hacker” a wonderfully accurate pseudo-demographic description of the people in hacker culture. But he objected to Raymond’s tendency to editorialize, even to flame, and he found Steele’s cartoons sophomoric and out of place next to the dry, sophisticated humor of the text. He ended with a few of his favorite finds. One of them is the unit conversion

1 attoparsec per microfortnight≈1 inch per second 1\ \text{attoparsec per microfortnight} \approx 1\ \text{inch per second} 1 attoparsec per microfortnight≈1 inch per second

He also mentioned an example of the canonical use of canonical, and a definition of a “cuspy but bogus raving story about N random broken people”.

Limitations#

Every design has trade-offs, and this one has at least four.

  1. A frozen snapshot. The file stopped growing after 1983 largely because the machines and communities behind it disappeared, and it stayed frozen for seven years. Its content was dated by the mid-1980s even as its legend grew.
  2. Report versus enshrine. The 1990 rewrite widened the file to cover more cultures, and that came at the cost of the older ones. Critics said Unix culture crowded out the file’s origins, and Raymond’s reply was that reporting was the point. Neither side is wrong. It is a design choice with a cost.
  3. The editor is in the data. Accusations that Raymond added terms reflecting his own politics and vocabulary, and Jackson’s criticism of editorializing, point at the same limitation. A dictionary compiled by people inside a culture cannot separate the observers from the observed. Raymond’s live-use check is a mitigation, not a proof.
  4. The trail has soft spots. The account of Crispin’s 1976 copy, for one, is a claim that still lacks a citation. The record of who said what in a lab’s chat and files is uneven, so the details of the file’s earliest years should be held a little loosely.

As of January 2016, the official file had not been updated since 2003. A volunteer editor produced two updates reflecting later influences from text-messaging language, LOLspeak and Internet slang in general. Those influences were mostly excoriated, and the last update came in January 2012. The idea that a living slang can be pinned down in one file has always been in tension with the fact that slang keeps moving.
