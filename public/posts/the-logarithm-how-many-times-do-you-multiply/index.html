The Logarithm: How Many Times Do You Multiply?

Logarithms aren't 'the inverse of exponentiation'. They're the answer to a much more natural question: how many multiplications did it take to get here?

Pick a number, say 102410241024, and ask a child’s question about it: how many times do I have to double 111 to get here?

Not “what’s the formula.” Just: how many doublings? Count them: 1,2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256,512,10241, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 10241,2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256,512,1024. Ten doublings. That number, 101010, is already a logarithm. It is log⁡21024\log_2 1024log2​1024, and you found it before writing a single symbol.

Everything below is what happens when you take that question seriously.

Start with the question, not the formula#

Take a base bbb and a target xxx. Ask: how many times must I multiply bbb by itself to reach xxx? Call the answer yyy. That is the entire definition:

log⁡bx=y  ⟺  by=x \log_b x = y \iff b^{y} = x logb​x=y⟺by=x

Read it as “log, base bbb, of xxx”. A few examples show how far the idea stretches:

Why does this question always have exactly one answer?#

For “how many doublings?” to be a fair question, every target needs one clear answer: never zero answers, never two. That isn’t automatic. It has to be earned.

  1. Take f(x)=bxf(x) = b^xf(x)=bx with b>1b > 1b>1. It is continuous and always increasing, so it never turns back on itself.
  2. Because it is continuous, it passes through every positive number on its way up.
  3. Because it is strictly increasing, it passes through each one exactly once.

The same argument works for 0<b<10 < b < 10<b<1, where the function is strictly decreasing. That is what licenses calling the logarithm the inverse of exponentiation, not just an inverse.

The one identity everything else builds on#

Multiply two numbers, and you add their “doubling counts”:

log⁡b(xy)=log⁡bx+log⁡by \log_b(xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y logb​(xy)=logb​x+logb​y

This is not a coincidence to memorize. It is the whole reason logarithms were invented. Before calculators, this single line turned a night of hand multiplication into two table lookups and an addition.

The other rules are the same idea in disguise#

A power is repeated multiplication, and a root is a fractional power, so every other rule follows from the product rule.

Switching bases without a new table#

Any base can borrow another base’s logarithm:

log⁡bx=log⁡kxlog⁡kb \log_b x = \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} logb​x=logk​blogk​x​

This is why a calculator only needs two log buttons, base 101010 and base eee, to compute a logarithm in any base you ask for.

Three bases, three reasons#

  • Base 101010 (the common logarithm, written lg⁡\lglg) matches how we write numbers, so it is the natural fit for hand computation and for reading off digit counts.
  • Base e≈2.71828e \approx 2.71828e≈2.71828 (the natural logarithm, written ln⁡\lnln) is the base whose logarithm has the simplest possible derivative. That is why calculus, analysis and physics live in base eee almost exclusively.
  • Base 222 (the binary logarithm, written lb⁡\operatorname{lb}lb) matches how computers, information and musical octaves are built.

A free fact: counting digits#

For a positive integer xxx, the number of digits is:

⌊log⁡10x⌋+1 \lfloor \log_{10} x \rfloor + 1 ⌊log10​x⌋+1

Take 598659865986: log⁡105986≈3.78\log_{10} 5986 \approx 3.78log10​5986≈3.78, the floor is 333, and adding 111 gives 444. The number 598659865986 does have 444 digits. The logarithm was quietly counting digits the whole time.

Why base e earns the word “natural”#

Here is the derivation that actually earns the name. The derivative of log⁡bx\log_b xlogb​x is 1xln⁡b\dfrac{1}{x \ln b}xlnb1​. That is messy, unless b=eb = eb=e, where ln⁡b=1\ln b = 1lnb=1 and everything collapses:

ddxln⁡x=1x \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x} dxd​lnx=x1​

That single fact lets us define ln⁡\lnln without mentioning exponentials at all. It is simply the area under the curve 1/x1/x1/x, measured from 111 to ttt:

ln⁡t=∫1t1x dx \ln t = \int_1^{t} \frac{1}{x}\,dx lnt=∫1t​x1​dx

From this geometric picture, the product rule falls out as an area-rescaling argument:

  1. Split the region from 111 to tututu into two pieces: one from 111 to ttt, and one from ttt to tututu.
  2. Squeeze the second piece horizontally by a factor of 1/t1/t1/t and stretch it vertically by a factor of ttt. The area doesn’t change, because the two effects cancel.
  3. That rescaled piece is now exactly the region from 111 to uuu.

Written out:

ln⁡(tu)=∫1tu1x dx=∫1t1x dx+∫ttu1x dx=ln⁡t+ln⁡u \begin{aligned} \ln(tu) &= \int_1^{tu} \frac{1}{x}\,dx \\ &= \int_1^{t} \frac{1}{x}\,dx + \int_t^{tu} \frac{1}{x}\,dx \\ &= \ln t + \ln u \end{aligned} ln(tu)​=∫1tu​x1​dx=∫1t​x1​dx+∫ttu​x1​dx=lnt+lnu​

Two regions, added areas, one logarithm identity, and no exponentiation anywhere.

A bonus connection: the harmonic series 1+12+13+⋯+1n1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots + \tfrac1n1+21​+31​+⋯+n1​ grows like ln⁡n\ln nlnn. The gap between the two settles toward a specific number, γ≈0.5772\gamma \approx 0.5772γ≈0.5772, the Euler–Mascheroni constant. It shows up when analyzing algorithms such as quicksort.

What breaks in the complex plane#

Once xxx is allowed to be a complex number, the logarithm stops being a plain function. A complex number’s angle is only defined up to a full turn: add 2π2\pi2π and you are pointing at the same number. Its logarithm inherits that ambiguity:

log⁡z=ln⁡∣z∣+i(φ+2kπ),k∈Z \log z = \ln \lvert z \rvert + i(\varphi + 2k\pi), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z} logz=ln∣z∣+i(φ+2kπ),k∈Z

By convention we report one “principal” value, but the honest picture is infinitely many valid answers stacked on top of each other, stitched together along a discontinuity called a branch cut.

The asymmetry that secures the internet#

Inside a finite group, exponentiation (combining an element with itself over and over) is cheap to compute. Its inverse is the discrete logarithm: given the result, which exponent got me here? In the right groups, that is believed to be extremely hard to reverse.

That one-way street is the basis of Diffie–Hellman key exchange. Two strangers can agree on a shared secret over a public channel because computing forward is easy and computing backward isn’t.

Once you know what to look for, logarithmic scales are everywhere. They compress enormous ranges into numbers people can actually work with.

Before calculators: tables and sliding sticks#

Henry Briggs published the first base-10 logarithm table in 1617, right after Napier’s invention, covering 111 to 100010001000 to 14-digit precision.

A number’s logarithm splits into a whole-number part, the characteristic, which comes from counting digits, and a decimal part, the mantissa, which you look up in the table. To multiply two numbers, you add their table entries, then look up the sum in reverse.

The slide rule mechanized the same trick. Sliding one logarithmically marked scale against another turns multiplication into physically adding two lengths. It remained standard engineering equipment into the 1970s.

Computing logarithms without a table#

Modern methods lean on the calculus picture above. You can expand ln⁡(1+z)\ln(1+z)ln(1+z) as a power series, or use a faster-converging series built from the inverse hyperbolic tangent. For extreme precision, 400 to 1000+ decimal digits, the arithmetic–geometric mean of two numbers converges to a value directly tied to ln⁡x\ln xlnx.

At Los Alamos, working on the Manhattan Project, Richard Feynman devised a bit-by-bit method, later reused in the Connection Machine. It builds up a logarithm using only additions and a small precomputed table, with no multiplication at all.

Four centuries, one word#

  • c. 1600: Jost Bürgi independently develops progression-based tables with similar speedups, using a technique called prosthaphaeresis.
  • 1614: John Napier publishes Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio. He coins “logarithmus”, Greek for “ratio-number”, to simplify the calculations of navigators and astronomers.
  • 1617: Henry Briggs publishes the first base-10 (“common”) logarithm table.
  • 1647: Grégoire de Saint-Vincent, trying to find the area under a hyperbola, stumbles onto what later becomes the natural logarithm.
  • 1675: Leibniz adopts the notation “Log”, then ties it to an integral the following year.
  • 18th century: Euler formalizes the modern view, the logarithm as the inverse of the exponential function, and gives its base the name eee.
  • Later: Laplace credits logarithms with effectively doubling an astronomer’s productive lifespan, by turning months of hand calculation into days.

Logarithm-shaped relatives#

Once you see “logarithm as an inverse” everywhere, its cousins show up fast:

  • the matrix logarithm, which inverts the matrix exponential;
  • the ppp-adic logarithm;
  • the discrete logarithm from the cryptography section;
  • the double logarithm ln⁡(ln⁡x)\ln(\ln x)ln(lnx);
  • the iterated (super-)logarithm, which inverts tetration;
  • the Lambert WWW function, which inverts w↦weww \mapsto w e^{w}w↦wew;
  • the logit, which inverts the logistic function.
