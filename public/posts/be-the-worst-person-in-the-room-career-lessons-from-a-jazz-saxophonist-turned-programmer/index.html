Be the Worst Person in the Room: Career Lessons From a Jazz Saxophonist Turned Programmer

Seven career ideas from Chad Fowler's The Passionate Programmer, from why you should be the weakest player in the band to why your career is a project you can't plan up front.

Here is career advice that sounds like sabotage: be the worst person in the room.

Chad Fowler was a professional jazz and blues saxophonist before he moved into software, and he built a whole book on lessons like that one. The Passionate Programmer is written for people who code, but nearly every idea works for anyone whose skills can go stale. These are the seven I’d keep.

1. Be the worst person in the room#

Your skills drift toward the level of the people around you, so choose them on purpose.

Fowler picked up a rule from jazz guitarist Pat Metheny: “Always be the worst guy in every band you’re in.” Standing next to musicians he admired, he was sure he’d be thrown off the bandstand. Instead, his playing started to match theirs, the way you pick up an accent after a few weeks abroad. The reverse also held. In weak bar bands he played like those bands, and the bad habits followed him to better gigs.

There was a second surprise. He wasn’t as bad as he feared, and he had proof: good musicians only call you back if they want to play with you again, and they kept calling. Aiming for the bottom stopped him from selling himself short.

Share this: if you can’t switch teams, join an open-source project you admire or a local developer meetup, and spend time with people a level above you.

2. Practice should sound ugly#

Practicing and performing are different jobs, and most of us only do the second.

Fowler remembers the music-school practice rooms: ugly sounds through thin walls, from players who were very good. Practice is supposed to sound bad because you’re working at the edge of your ability. Athletes do their straining behind closed doors, too. Software, he argues, is the odd profession where people practice on the job, in public, on the employer’s time.

His fix was to write software nobody needed, so there was no pressure to cut corners. The results were awkward, and he came to read the awkwardness as a good sign. If only elegant code comes out of a practice session, you’re sitting near the center of what you can already do.

Share this: pick one dusty corner of your main tool (he suggests regular expressions, a compact way to describe text patterns) and drill it on a throwaway project. Then read other people’s code the way a studio musician sight-reads a new score.

3. Treat your skills like an investment portfolio#

Every skill you learn is a bet, and bets have risk and reward.

Fowler runs a thought experiment about three technologies. Learning COBOL, an old business language, was safe: work was easy to find, but it didn’t pay spectacularly. Learning Java early was risky, since few employers used it at first, but it could pay very well for those who got in early. Learning BeOS, a dazzling new operating system, was risky and paid nothing: it never caught on, and the developers who mastered it never got their investment back.

Then he adds a twist. Old systems don’t die, they get replaced in stages, and during the handover the old and new have to talk to each other. Young developers don’t know the old systems and veterans don’t know the new ones. That gap is a job he calls technology hospice: be the person still able to support critical systems after everyone else has moved on, and you hold the leverage. The risk is real, because one day you’ll be an expert in something that no longer exists. His answer is to move fast and find the next fading system.

Share this: draw a line on paper, newest tools on the left and sunsetting ones on the right. Mark where your skills sit. If they clump in one spot, that’s your exposure.

4. You can’t win on price, so build robots#

If you compete on hourly cost, someone cheaper will always appear.

Fowler tells the story of early web design. When websites were new, companies paid thousands of dollars for a basic one, and he gives 5,000astheexample.Peoplenoticed,learnedHTML,andthemarketflooded.Soonthesamecompanieswouldpay5,000 as the example. People noticed, learned HTML, and the market flooded. Soon the same companies would pay 5,000astheexample.Peoplenoticed,learnedHTML,andthemarketflooded.Soonthesamecompanieswouldpay500. Offshore programming, he argues, injected a large supply of low-cost labor, and you can’t afford to fight on price.

Managers can’t easily measure how fast a programmer is, so they count what they can: cost per hour. That leaves three ways to produce more software: faster people, more people, or automation. Faster is hard to prove, and he doesn’t want to encourage the cheap-army approach. Automation is the one left. He points to the factory robots of the 1980s: everyone was upset except the people who built the robots.

Share this: pick one task you repeat, and write a small program that does it for you. Start simple and don’t worry about reuse.

5. The boring job may be the freest one#

Low expectations are a kind of freedom.

When Fowler helped set up a 250-person development center from scratch, he hit a surprise. Everyone wanted to build new systems and nobody wanted to maintain old ones. Maintenance looked low-status and messy, and new projects looked like the road to promotion.

His coffee analogy flips it. Give someone $1,000 and ask for a coffee, and you’ll be furious if they return with neither money nor coffee. Give them nothing and ask for a coffee, and you’ll be grateful if it shows up and understanding if it doesn’t. Project work is the first case: well funded, closely watched, expected to deliver. Maintenance is the second: nobody expects anything flashy, customers stay hands-off, and a bug that forces a redesign of part of the system is just part of fixing bugs. For a creative person, that can be the more open playground.

Share this: next time you want to dodge the unglamorous task, ask what you could do with it precisely because nobody is watching.

6. Work like you only have eight hours#

Scarce time gets used better than plentiful time.

The Extreme Programming movement, a style of software teamwork, says people should work no more than forty hours a week. That upset managers and many programmers too, for whom long hours were a badge of toughness. Bob Martin, a well-known voice in that movement, renamed the idea the eight-hour burn: work so relentlessly that you couldn’t possibly continue past eight hours.

Fowler’s argument has two parts. Tired people make mistakes that cost time and money, and projects are marathons, not sprints. And your hours behave like money: when you have seventy to spend, each one feels cheap. By day four of a seventy-hour week, it’s 10:30 a.m., you know you’ll be there long after everyone else leaves, and you start reading tech news. A hard stop makes you rank your tasks and attack them one at a time.

Share this: set a firm finish time tomorrow, list your tasks in priority order before you start, and see how much of the list is done when the clock hits it.

7. Don’t plan your career like a waterfall project#

You can’t know up front what you’ll want, so a ten-year blueprint will fail for the same reason big software plans do.

In the old way of building software, called waterfall, analysts write huge specifications, architects hand down designs, and after months or years the result arrives, thoroughly tested and not what anyone wanted. A group of developers rebelled and created the agile family of methods, built around the assumption that change is constant. Fowler later realized he had planned his own career like a waterfall project: newbie programmer, architect, manager, director, on track to become a vice president or chief information officer. The more he succeeded, the less he liked the work. He was efficiently building a career he didn’t want.

The fix was to change it, and for him that meant returning to hands-on technology. He pairs this with a warning about value rigidity: beliefs you hold so firmly you never test them. Like a frog in slowly heating water, you don’t notice the change. Many network administrators in the mid-1990s swore by Novell’s NetWare. No single event ended it, and by the time they noticed, the water was boiling.

Share this: make two columns, career and technology, and list the beliefs you never question. Then ask which ones you would drop if the evidence changed.

The honey#

Your career is the biggest project you will ever run, so run it like a good team runs software: get feedback from people better than you, practice at your limits, automate the repetitive parts, and change course the moment the plan stops fitting.

  • Spend this month somewhere you’re the weakest player.
  • Schedule one practice session that is allowed to sound bad.
  • Map your skills on the new-to-sunsetting line.
  • Automate one task you repeat every week.
  • Write down one career belief you’ve never questioned, and challenge it.

One caveat#

This is a book of experience and opinion, not research. Fowler backs most points with stories from his own career, and the eight-hour burn and be-the-worst ideas rest on his reasoning, not on studies. His examples also come from 2009. The technologies he names, such as J2EE and .NET, and his worries about offshore competition belong to that moment, and technology hospice is a gamble he admits himself. Keep the habits and treat the specifics as dated.

Source: Chad Fowler, The Passionate Programmer: Creating a Remarkable Career in Software Development (2009).
