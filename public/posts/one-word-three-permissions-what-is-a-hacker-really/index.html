One Word, Three Permissions: What Is a Hacker, Really?

Trace the word 'hacker' step by step, from a railway-club joke to a job title, and see why the same skills can mean three very different things.

Let’s start with a bug report, because that is what the word “hacker” is. Two people say “hacker” and mean opposite things, and both are using the word correctly. So we won’t argue about which meaning is right. We’ll find out what differs between the meanings, and what stays the same.

The problem, stated precisely#

Here is a claim we can test. The word names one set of skills: finding out how a system works, then making it do something its designers didn’t plan. If that’s true, the meanings should differ only in who is allowed to do it, and why.

We can write that as a function:

function classify(person):
    skill      = can_explore_and_bend_systems(person)     # shared by every sense
    permission = has_authorization(person)                # the variable that changes
    purpose    = why_they_do_it(person)                   # so does this
    community  = who_calls_them_a_hacker(person)          # and this

    return meaning_of_hacker(skill, permission, purpose, community)

Only the last three inputs change. The overlap in skill and method is real. What differs is authorization, purpose and community identity. Keep those three in mind while we trace the history.

Step 1: Where does the word come from?#

The verb hack is very old English, and it originally meant cutting something with repeated blows. A “hacker” was someone who did that, centuries before electronic computers existed.

The computing meaning grew out of student slang at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The first edition of the Tech Model Railroad Club’s dictionary, compiled in 1959, defined a “hack” as a project with no constructive purpose, and a “hacker” as the person doing it. Before the word was tied to computers, it meant playful tinkering and pranks involving machinery.

A darker sense showed up almost immediately. In 1963, MIT’s student newspaper, The Tech, used “telephone hackers” for students who disrupted phone service and made unauthorized long-distance calls. The legal scholar Fred R. Shapiro documents both uses at MIT before the word reached the wider public: harmless tinkering and unauthorized interference, side by side from the start.

So the ambiguity is in the original code. Nobody introduced it later.

Step 2: What do the dictionaries say now?#

Current dictionaries list two main computing senses:

  1. An expert in programming and computer problem-solving.
  2. More commonly, a person who tries to gain, or does gain, unauthorized access to computer systems.

Programmer and free-software communities tend to use the word warmly, for people who enjoy exploring programmable systems, modifying software and finding clever routes around technical limits. In computer security it covers lawful penetration testers and experts, and also people acting without permission. Outside security it can mean an enthusiast or expert in some other field, or anyone who works around a practical limitation.

Some programmer communities try to settle this by reserving “hacker” for the enthusiast and using “cracker” for someone who breaks into systems maliciously or illegally. That split appeared in the 1980s as a reaction to negative media coverage, and it is still disputed, because lawful and unlawful hacking can use the same techniques. General dictionaries still list unauthorized access as a standard meaning of “hacker”.

Vulnerabilities are found and reported by researchers, developers, system administrators, users, academics, security companies and government organizations. Not all of them call themselves “hackers” or “security researchers”.

Step 3: A vocabulary table#

Security work added a whole family of labels. Most of them classify people by authorization and intent, and their edges stay disputed.

Term What it means
Ethical hacker / white hat A security professional who tests systems lawfully
Black hat Someone who acts illicitly or without sanction
Gray hat May act without permission, but without the malicious intent usually attributed to black hats
Cybercriminal Someone who commits crimes through computers or the Internet
Script kiddie An informal, disparaging term for a low-skill intruder who relies on programs written by others
Phreaker Someone who gains illegal access to, or manipulates, telephone systems
Hacktivist Someone who uses hacking to pursue political, social, religious or ideological goals
Cyberterrorism Operations meant to cause grave harm, widespread fear, loss of life or severe damage to essential systems

Step 4: Trace the history#

Here is the timeline as a trace, in the order things actually happened.

1958  TX-0 at MIT: a hacker community forms around it (from about this year)
1959  TMRC dictionary defines "hack" and "hacker"
1962  PDP-1 installed at MIT; the community expands
1963  The Tech uses "telephone hackers" (negative sense)
1973  Community Memory opens in Berkeley: first public computer bulletin board
1975  Homebrew Computer Club founded
1976  Bill Gates publishes an open letter to hobbyists
1983  WarGames reaches a mass audience
1984  GNU Project begins; Steven Levy coins "hacker ethic"
1986  Computer Fraud and Abuse Act enacted (US)
1988  Morris worm
1990  Operation Sundevil; Electronic Frontier Foundation founded (July)
1993  DEF CON first held; Bugtraq mailing list created
1997  Black Hat conference established
1998  L0pht Heavy Industries testifies before the US Senate
2016  Hack the Pentagon: first federal bug-bounty program

The MIT lab years#

Members of the Tech Model Railroad Club learned about switching circuits and logical control from the relay systems that ran their model railway. Some later experimented with the computers MIT made available to students.

Most computers of the period were run by specialist staff. The TX-0 and, from 1962, the PDP-1 could be used directly by members of the MIT community, and a hacker community formed around them. It had links to Project MAC and the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, and its members wrote experimental programs, including a screen-based text editor and Spacewar!. In 1984 the journalist Steven Levy used the phrase “hacker ethic” for the values he associated with this community.

Computing leaves the university#

In the 1970s, cheaper microprocessors and personal-computer kits put computing within reach of hobbyists outside universities, government laboratories and big businesses. Community Memory opened in Berkeley in 1973 and gave people with no previous computer access a place to exchange messages. The Homebrew Computer Club, founded in 1975, was a forum where hobbyists and experimenters shared ideas and demonstrated new hardware and software.

In 1976, unauthorized copies of Altair BASIC were circulating. Microsoft’s founder, Bill Gates, criticized hobbyists in an open letter. He argued that copying deprived programmers of payment and discouraged anyone from producing commercial software. So the tension between sharing and ownership is as old as the hobbyist scene.

The personal-computer movement then carried hacker culture into commercial software and the home video-game industry. In 1984 the GNU Project began organizing the development of a Unix-compatible operating system whose software could be run, copied, modified and redistributed. GNU components were later combined with the Linux kernel into a complete free operating system.

The underground#

The computer underground emerged in the 1980s from telephone phreaking and from users exploring computer systems outside institutions. As home computers and modems spread, bulletin board systems linked participants in different cities. They traded technical information, login credentials, software and news. Groups formed and published electronic magazines, and the Legion of Doom and Phrack became well-known examples.

Participants explored, learned technical methods, competed with each other and chased peer recognition. Some drew a line between that activity and payment-card fraud or destructive attacks, but there was no agreed boundary. A 1986 essay, the Hacker Manifesto, is associated with this underground.

The public turns#

During the 1980s, reports of arrests and intrusions made “hacker” increasingly mean a digital trespasser. Programmers kept using it positively inside technical culture. The 1983 film WarGames brought computer intrusion to a mass audience and helped present hackers as a threat to national security.

Then the law arrived:

  • 1986: The US Congress enacted the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, creating federal offenses for specified forms of unauthorized access to protected computers.
  • 1988: The Morris worm disrupted a substantial part of the early Internet. Its creator, Robert Tappan Morris, was convicted in 1990, the first conviction obtained under the act.
  • 1990: Operation Sundevil and related Secret Service investigations targeted bulletin-board operators and people suspected of telecommunications and computer offenses. The raids and seizures raised questions about speech, privacy and the legal treatment of electronic communications. The Electronic Frontier Foundation was founded in July of that year, after an investigation involving the publication of a telephone-system document, and it provided legal help in cases involving computer users and electronic communication.

The professional turn#

In the 1990s, people from hacker and underground communities began working in security consulting, software companies and corporate research groups, because their knowledge of vulnerabilities had become commercially useful.

DEF CON (first held in 1993) and Black Hat (established in 1997) put independent hackers in contact with security firms, vendors, government personnel and law-enforcement officers. The Bugtraq mailing list, created in 1993, gave vulnerability finders a public place for technical disclosures. It let them build professional reputations and pressured vendors to fix reported flaws. In 1998, members of L0pht Heavy Industries testified before the US Senate as security experts rather than criminal defendants.

Coordinated-disclosure policies and bug bounty programs later gave researchers formal channels for reporting vulnerabilities and working with affected organizations. In 2016 the US Department of Defense ran Hack the Pentagon, the first bug-bounty program in the federal government. Eligible participants tested selected public websites under defined rules.

Even after all this professionalization, the word kept both meanings. It described trusted security specialists and people regarded as threats.

Step 5: The traditions, one at a time#

Now we can separate the sub-communities. They aren’t one group.

Programmer and free-software hackers. Here, hacking means understanding and modifying software, including technically inventive ways to make a computer behave as intended or do something unexpected. Free-software projects use licenses that let users study, modify and redistribute programs. Internet-based projects often draw contributors from many places, release early versions for testing and fold in corrections from users and programmers. Open licenses let anyone alter the code, but established projects usually have maintainers who decide which changes enter the official version. Open-source communities disagree with each other about proprietary software and the role of commercial businesses. Free-software hackers stress continuing software freedom, while open-source hackers put more weight on development methods and freedom of choice in how software is used.

Hackerspaces. These host programming, device building and other experimentation with digital technology, and hacking here usually means constructive technical work. A hackerspace is a community-run physical place where people work on projects and learn from one another, governed by local rules and values about shared space and technology.

Hardware hackers. Open-source hardware provides design files that others can study, modify, manufacture and redistribute. A design is not yet an object, though: it still needs components, materials and manufacturing. Hardware can also contain weaknesses that are exploitable through physical or remote access.

Phreakers. They explore and manipulate telephone networks and the technologies that control calls. In the 1970s this meant technical experimentation, pranks, avoiding call charges, and campaigns that treated the Bell System as a target of political protest. Phreaking publications and practices later influenced parts of the computer underground.

The underground. It held exploratory intruders and people engaged in fraud, theft or deliberate damage, and members disagreed about where hacking ended and criminal abuse began.

Security hackers. They have the expertise to find or exploit weaknesses in systems, and the label alone doesn’t say whether the activity is authorized or malicious. Authorized penetration testers imitate attacks within an agreed scope, demonstrate vulnerabilities and report findings to the organization responsible for the system. Unauthorized ones may seek personal gain or other malicious aims. Some attackers, including state-sponsored groups, run long campaigns against chosen organizations or governments for political, strategic or commercial goals.

Hacktivists. They use computer skills and digital action to advance political or social causes. Their activities include distributing information, building tools for political campaigns, defacing websites, disclosing information without authorization and interrupting online services. Ordinary online advocacy isn’t usually counted as hacktivism unless it involves direct technical intervention or a contentious action against a chosen target. Hacktivism has been described as a digital counterpart of civil disobedience, but unauthorized disruption or access may still be treated as a crime whatever the political purpose. News reports and government statements have sometimes lumped hacktivism together with cyberterrorism, or treated politically motivated hackers mainly as security threats.

Step 6: What do hackers value, and how do they organize?#

Accounts of early MIT hacker culture stress access to computers, information sharing, decentralization, and status earned through technical work rather than formal rank. Later traditions also value technical workmanship and clever ways around constraints. The anthropologist Gabriella Coleman notes that hackers may judge work by its quality or elegance and may prize humor and playfulness.

These communities disagree about software freedom, privacy, commercial work and political action. Coleman links civil-liberties and anti-authoritarian commitments to many Western hackers, but limits libertarianism to particular regions and projects.

The motives people report are worth a look:

Survey Finding
2003, 684 free and open-source developers Enjoyment and creativity were the strongest motives; respondents also cited user need, intellectual stimulation and skill development; about two-fifths were paid for their work
2021 Learning and intellectual stimulation stayed important; social connection, reputation, career advancement, enjoyment and helping others varied with experience

Hackers often learn by working directly on systems and swapping knowledge with peers. The channels include mailing lists, chat channels, code repositories, forums, electronic publications and shared workshops, used for questions, code review, publication and testing. Large free-software projects may have maintainers, membership procedures, contributor review and voting. Elite security groups have sometimes limited sensitive information to trusted members who had shown technical ability.

Reputation works differently on the security side. Standing comes from visible technical contributions and peer recognition. A pseudonymous handle hides a legal identity while preserving credit and group affiliation for tools, exploits or publications. As professional security work grew, some hackers began using their legal names so disclosures and tools could support résumés, jobs and public credibility. Public credit also caused disputes over private knowledge, reused code and work claimed by someone else.

Security meetups and conferences bring together people who otherwise interact at a distance, and newcomers learn techniques and meet the wider scene. Programs mix lectures and technical demonstrations with project work, informal discussion, games and social events. Some events are run by volunteer communities, while others are tied to companies, government agencies or professional security work. Free-software projects hold their own meetings for development, governance and community business. Meeting in person strengthens relationships otherwise kept up online.

Who gets in?#

Formal openness doesn’t remove informal barriers. In security groups, membership and access to private knowledge may depend on skill, trust, contribution history and familiarity with group norms. In free-culture settings, meritocratic ideals can sit alongside narrow ideas of the “real hacker”, adversarial discussion and informal personal networks.

Gender imbalance is well documented in free and open-source software. A review of 51 publications estimated that women made up about 9.8% of contributors, and named low peer parity and non-inclusive communication among the main social barriers. Conferences and online communities also report sexist remarks, harassment and exclusion. Women in the security scene describe support from peers, along with encounters where others ignored or doubted their technical interest. Responses have included women-focused initiatives and clearer conduct or governance measures. Feminist hackerspaces offer places for technical learning, skill sharing and community work, and some address race, sexuality, gender identity and class as well as women’s underrepresentation.

Step 7: The authorization protocol#

Back to the permission variable in our function. In practice it isn’t a feeling. It’s a written boundary.

Before a professional security test, rules of engagement usually specify the authorized activities and their limits. A public vulnerability-disclosure policy authorizes independent research only on the systems and methods it covers. The US Department of Justice’s policy permits only enough testing to confirm a vulnerability:

ALLOWED   confirm that the vulnerability exists
FORBIDDEN remove data
FORBIDDEN maintain access after testing
FORBIDDEN gain higher privileges
FORBIDDEN move into connected systems
FORBIDDEN install malware
FORBIDDEN disrupt services
IF UNSURE ask for clarification before continuing

Permission from one organization doesn’t extend to systems, information or services controlled by third parties, and complying with a disclosure policy doesn’t excuse unrelated violations of law.

There is also a prosecutorial layer. US Department of Justice charging policy tells federal prosecutors to decline prosecution under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act when the evidence shows good-faith security research. It defines that as testing, investigating or correcting a security flaw in a way designed to avoid harm, with the findings used mainly to improve security or safety. But this is internal guidance for federal prosecutors. It creates no legal right that a researcher can enforce.

Tracing a coordinated disclosure#

In coordinated vulnerability disclosure, a finder reports a flaw and works with vendors, coordinators or other affected parties before publishing anything. Here is the flow:

1. Finder reports the flaw
     report should identify: affected product or configuration
                             possible impact
                             steps to reproduce
                             evidence or a working demonstration
2. Recipient confirms (validates) and prioritizes the flaw
3. Recipient decides how urgently to address it
4. Repair is developed and tested
5. Public notice: the vulnerability and remediation plan are published
6. A fix or mitigation is deployed

Two words in that flow need care. Remediation removes a vulnerability. Mitigation reduces its effect without removing the underlying flaw. A reliable patch can take time, especially when the vulnerable code is reused across products or supplied by another vendor. The US Department of Labor’s policy tells researchers to stop testing once they’ve confirmed a flaw or met sensitive data, and to allow time for remediation before publishing technical details.

Step 8: What the law says about touching software#

Copyright generally protects the expression of a computer program. It does not protect the program’s ideas, procedures, methods of operation or mathematical concepts. In the European Union, copyright holders control reproduction, alteration and public distribution of programs, with stated exceptions.

Free-software licenses give users the freedom to run, study, modify, copy and redistribute programs. Copyleft licenses require redistributed versions to keep those freedoms, and free software can also use licenses without that requirement.

EU law lets users observe and test software in some circumstances. A lawful user may also translate necessary portions of compiled code into another form when that is essential to make an independently created program work with other software, and the information must not otherwise be available. What’s learned this way can’t be used for unrelated purposes, shared beyond what compatibility needs, or used to build a substantially similar infringing program. The directive also requires remedies against commercial dealing in tools intended solely to bypass technical protections on software.

Step 9: Why does the public picture look so different?#

News coverage often uses “hacker” for an unauthorized intruder and ties the word to crime, security threats and disruption. A study of five US newspapers, covering the year before and after the September 11 attacks, found hackers increasingly framed as possible cyberterrorists. Earlier coverage more often treated them as ordinary computer criminals. Motives got little attention unless an article profiled a hacker, and reports blurred hacktivism with cyberterrorism even though the aims and level of harm differ.

National politics matter too. A comparison of China Daily and The New York Times from 2001 to 2020 found that both gave states a prominent role in hacker coverage, and each framed accusations and foreign governments from its own national and political angle. Positive references grew over time, especially around ethical hackers and defensive security, but associations with hostile activity remained.

Visuals follow a pattern too: dark rooms, hidden faces, hoods, masks and screens full of code. James Shires calls this imagery “cyber-noir”, a hidden, morally uncertain world where anonymous threats and specialists may use similar methods for different ends.

The screen hacker, decade by decade#

Era Portrayal
1980s WarGames (1983) and early hacking games: gifted but socially isolated young people entering powerful systems from home computers and swept into events beyond ordinary life
1990s Hackers (1995): a stylish subcultural rebel; The Matrix (1999): that figure turned into a hero resisting an oppressive digital system
2000s–2010s Swordfish (2001) and Blackhat (2015): hackers in stories of terrorism, surveillance, cybercrime and international conflict, as dangerous offenders, necessary allies, or both
2015–19 Mr. Robot: the alienated hacker again, with more psychological depth, tied to mental illness, surveillance, corporate power, political anger and the personal consequences of technical action; it used recognizable tools and plausible attack methods rather than treating every intrusion as visual spectacle

Games let players perform simplified intrusions and pick targets or tools, in the role of a criminal, mercenary, activist or defender.

How accurate is the screen version? A sample of 50 film and TV scenes released between 2010 and 2022 showed attacks finishing quickly and succeeding at unusually high rates, with the technical process usually hidden inside a simplified “black box”.

Step 10: The word escapes#

The favorable sense of “hack”, an inventive way to solve a problem, has spread well beyond programming and security:

  • Hackathon: a short event where programmers, often joined by people from other specialties, work intensively to produce code or a prototype. Hackathons are also used to build scientific collaborations and communities, or to prototype answers to urgent problems.
  • Civic hacking: applying technical and design skills to public problems, often through government data or redesigned public services. Participants include programmers, designers, data specialists, organizers, entrepreneurs and government employees, and the label doesn’t imply unauthorized access.
  • Biohacking: in do-it-yourself biology, people run biological experiments outside conventional academic or industrial laboratories. The term is also used for health and wellness practices, technological augmentation, and self-directed experiments on the body or mind.
  • Growth hacking: iterative, data-driven experimentation meant to help an organization scale and adapt, used beyond startups and marketing.
  • Life hack: a simple tip or technique for doing an ordinary task more easily or efficiently.

Limitations: what this model can’t do#

Our classify function is tidy, but the tidiness isn’t quite honest:

  • The variables aren’t cleanly separable. The hat terms sort people by authorization and intent, yet their boundaries were disputed as security work moved from underground groups into professional settings. The “hacker versus cracker” split is contested for the same reason: the techniques are the same either way.
  • Communities don’t agree on who may use the label. Inside computing, people disagree over which uses of “hacker” are legitimate.
  • Authorization is narrower than it looks. It can be scoped to specific systems and methods, and it doesn’t carry over to third parties.
  • Prosecutorial restraint isn’t a legal right. The US charging policy is guidance, not a shield.
  • Professionalization didn’t settle the word. It left “hacker” meaning both a trusted specialist and a suspected threat.

So the honest answer to “what is a hacker?” is a function with unresolved inputs. Ask who gave permission, what they were trying to do, and who is doing the naming.
